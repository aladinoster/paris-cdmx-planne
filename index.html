    How plans are compared

    Every plan maximises one number, the score: the value of our days together minus everything they cost. Value per day is the exchange rate between time and money. Raise it and the optimiser buys more days; lower it and it saves money.

    How to use this, my love (30 seconds)
    1. The three rows of buttons below are questions for the two of us. Each answer changes the plan.
    2. Tap a button and everything re-optimises instantly: the trips, the calendar, the totals and the countdown highlights.
    3. The buttons marked recommended are already selected. That’s the plan I’d pick for us: realistic prices, balanced between time and money, any destination allowed.
    4. No combination is “wrong”. Let’s look at the days together and the cost range each one gives, and keep the one that feels right to us.

    The question

    Choose the trips of 2027 (start day, end day, who flies or which city, and whether the host also takes leave) to make the year as good as possible together. The constraints are both leave budgets, Laura’s blocked days and the fixed trips.

    Formulation

    maximise   Σ trips [ Σ days V · w(d)  +  F  −  κ · m · ( fare + hotel + C ) ]
           − Σ gaps  λ · max(0, g − g*)²

subject to Σ leave_A ≤ 45      Σ leave_L ≤ 22
           trips don’t overlap, gaps ≥ 14 days
           no leave for L on her last working day of a month
             nor her first 3 of the next (weekends skipped), nor in August
           every fixed trip starts/ends in its window
           (optional) one trip with ≥ ½ of its days in April

w(d) = 1 (both free) · β (host working) · ½ (travel day)
κ = 0.6  weight of money        F = €100 per trip (frequency)
m = 0.7  meeting halfway, 1 at home

    It’s a resource-constrained scheduling problem: a knapsack with two budgets (the leave days) on a timeline, plus a convex penalty on gaps.

    Solver

    • Exact dynamic programming over the 365 days. The state is (day, next fixed trip, Andrés’s leave left, Laura’s leave left), about 1.9 M states. Each candidate trip is one transition, and there are tens of thousands of them.
    • The leave dimensions are handled as whole arrays at once, so a full solve takes ~0.3 s on a phone. This page runs it in JavaScript; the meetplan Python package runs the same model with numpy.
    • Optimality is proven, not hoped for. A test enumerates every feasible plan of a small instance and checks the DP finds the same optimum. The JS and Python solvers agree to the cent.
    • A MILP or CP-SAT solver (OR-Tools) would also work, but this DP is exact, needs no server and runs instantly in your browser.

    Uncertainty

    • For each month and direction, the lowest fare seen across Kiwi, lastminute and LetsFG is the base. A ticket you actually buy is base × m, with m ~ Triangular(1.00, 1.08, 1.30). The 1.08 is the spread between sites on identical dates; the 1.30 covers direct flights or booking at a bad moment.
    • The optimiser plans with the expected fare (×1.13) by default. “Cheapest seen” and “Cautious” (90th percentile, ×1.22) show how sensitive the plan is to prices.
    • Each plan is then priced 4,000 times with random fares (Monte Carlo) to give an 80% cost range.
